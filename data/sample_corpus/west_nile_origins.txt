West Nile Virus — Origins and Early History

West Nile virus (WNV) is a mosquito-borne flavivirus.

First isolation: 1937, West Nile district of Uganda, from the blood of a febrile woman during yellow-fever surveillance (Smithburn et al.).

Natural cycle: Birds (especially passerines) serve as amplifying hosts; Culex mosquitoes are the primary vectors. Humans and horses are incidental (dead-end) hosts.

Pre-1999 distribution: Africa, Middle East, parts of Europe and Asia. Mostly mild febrile illness; occasional neurological disease.

Notable early outbreaks:
- Israel, early 1950s (first well-documented epidemic).
- Romania 1996 and other European events in the mid-1990s marked a shift toward more severe neuroinvasive disease.

Introduction to the Western Hemisphere: 1999, New York City area. The North American strain is closely related to a 1998 Israeli isolate. The exact mechanism of trans-Atlantic introduction remains unknown (most plausible routes involve infected birds or mosquitoes transported by ship or aircraft).

There are no documented human outbreaks or isolations of West Nile virus in the early 1900s. Molecular-clock estimates place some lineage divergences earlier, but the virus was unknown to science before 1937.

Key references (public scientific literature):
- Smithburn et al., isolation 1937/1940.
- Roehrig, “West Nile Virus in the United States — A Historical Perspective” (Viruses, 2013).
- Hayes, historical reviews of Old-World ecology.
